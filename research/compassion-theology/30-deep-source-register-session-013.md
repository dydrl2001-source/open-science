# 30. Deep Source Register — Session 013: Acts 10–11–15

## A. Acts 10–11: Peter/Cornelius
### Narrative observations
- Acts 10:17: Peter is **διηπόρει** — deeply perplexed/at a loss about the vision’s meaning.
- 10:19: he continues pondering (διενθυμουμένου); interpretation is not instant.
- 10:20 / 11:12: Spirit commands him to go with Gentile messengers; the later retelling sharpens the sense toward “making no distinction.”
- 10:28: Peter himself interprets the vision relationally: God showed him not to call **any person** common/unclean.
- 10:34: **ἐπ’ ἀληθείας καταλαμβάνομαι** — “now/truly I grasp/perceive”; commentators note a new grasp of what had not previously been comprehended.
- 10:44–48: Spirit falls before Peter completes the expected ritual sequence; circumcised companions are astonished.
- 11:1–3: Jerusalem believers challenge Peter specifically over entering/eating with uncircumcised people.
- 11:4: Peter **ἐξετίθετο ... καθεξῆς** — explains/recounts the matter in ordered sequence.
- 11:12: six Jewish-Christian companions function as additional witnesses.
- 11:15–17: Peter interprets the event by remembering Jesus’ saying about Spirit baptism.
- 11:18: critics fall silent and glorify God, revising their judgment: God has granted repentance unto life to Gentiles.

### Scholarship
- Luke gives the Cornelius episode unusual narrative space and repeats it; scholars widely treat it as programmatically important for Gentile inclusion.
- Thomas O’Loughlin reads 10–11 strongly through table fellowship and boundary crossing.
- Zovkić emphasizes Peter’s first-hand experience plus six witnesses and the ordered apologia before Jerusalem.
- Luke Timothy Johnson, as cited in discernment scholarship, stresses that Peter responds to challenge not simply by apostolic authority but by narrating what happened.
- Caution: claims about what was “unlawful” for Jews, food-law abrogation, and exact historical Jewish-Gentile commensality remain debated.

## B. Acts 15: Jerusalem discernment
Narrative sequence:
1. 15:1,5 — concrete doctrinal/practical dispute: circumcision/Torah and Gentile salvation/belonging.
2. 15:6–7 — “much debate” (πολλῆς ζητήσεως).
3. 15:7–11 — Peter recalls God’s prior choice and Spirit-gift; God “made no distinction” and cleansed hearts by faith.
4. 15:12 — assembly listens to Barnabas and Paul narrate signs/wonders among Gentiles.
5. 15:13–18 — James interprets these events in relation to “the words of the prophets.”
6. 15:15 — **καὶ τούτῳ συμφωνοῦσιν οἱ λόγοι τῶν προφητῶν**: the prophetic words “agree/harmonize with this.”
7. 15:16–18 — Amos 9:11–12 in a form close to/adapted from LXX, with wider prophetic resonance.
8. 15:19–21 — James renders a practical judgment.
9. 15:22 — apostles, elders, and whole church participate in sending representatives.
10. 15:28 — “it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us.”

### Amos 9 debate
- MT and LXX differ significantly at Amos 9:12: “possess remnant of Edom” vs LXX-type “remnant of humanity may seek [the Lord].”
- Acts’ form is not simply identical to MT; scholars debate whether James/Luke follows LXX, an adapted/conflated scriptural form, or a Hebrew textual tradition underlying it.
- Therefore Acts 15 must not be presented as a simple modern proof of “experience changes Scripture.”
- Stronger and safer claim: Luke narrates recent divine action/testimony and prophetic Scripture as **consonant witnesses** within communal discernment.
- Some interpreters emphasize Scripture validating experience; others emphasize the experience leading the community to notice/read Scripture in a newly salient way.

## C. Stephen Fowl and theological interpretation
- *Engaging Scripture: A Model for Theological Interpretation* (1998), especially “How the Spirit Reads and How to Read the Spirit,” is a major conversation partner.
- Fowl-related scholarship uses Acts 10–15 to argue that experience of the Spirit shapes scriptural reading while Scripture provides lenses for discerning the Spirit’s work.
- This is close to our proposal and therefore is a **major prior-art risk**: we cannot claim novelty for a generic “experience ↔ Scripture ↔ Spirit ↔ community” model.
- Our possible distinct contribution must remain narrower: suffering/counter-testimony as stress test + Ricoeurian trial + Metzian interruption + normative return.

## D. Methodological warning
Acts narrates unique salvation-historical events. It is not automatically a repeatable decision algorithm.
We must distinguish:
- descriptive Lukan narrative,
- Luke’s theological emphasis,
- constructive ecclesial analogy today.
